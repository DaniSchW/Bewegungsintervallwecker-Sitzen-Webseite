Floor Sitting in Detail

On the homepage we introduced floor sitting as the position that performs best in the research. Here we go deeper: what sets it apart from other positions, how it fits into everyday life, and what the evidence across cultures suggests. Every claim links directly to its source.

What sets floor sitting apart from chairs, bar stools, and standing

The key difference isn't whether one position is "more active," but variety: on the floor you can shift between cross-legged sitting, kneeling, a deep squat, or outstretched legs without ever leaving your spot — a small shift in weight is enough.

read more…show less

With a chair, bar stool, or standing, on the other hand, a change of position almost always means switching furniture or the whole category. Historically, chair sitting also isn't a physically prescribed default: it tends to flatten the spine's natural curve and only became the cultural norm over time — not because it's anatomically necessary.

Source: Aktion Gesunder Rücken (AGR) e.V., Bewegte Schule: "Sitting on Chairs – The Origin Story of a Mass Phenomenon" (PDF)

Simple and inexpensive to adopt — even for kids

Floor sitting doesn't require any special furniture: a futon, a floor cushion (zafu), or a low table instead of standard dining height is enough. In countries like India, Japan, or Korea, sitting on the floor to eat is entirely normal.

read more…show less

Children naturally gravitate toward floor-level sitting postures on their own — a low table with cushions fits that natural preference better than a high chair with a footrest.

Source: Akutmag

Practical: barely any fall height

Floor furniture like cushions or a zafu has practically no fall height — an advantage over bar stools or tall chairs, especially in households with young children.

read more…show less

On smooth floors, a non-slip mat is worth using so cushions or seating surfaces don't slide out from under you.

Practical note — not a study, our own assessment.

Which muscles are activated by floor sitting?

To understand why this works, it helps to look at the muscles involved: without a backrest and without a fixed seat, the deep hip flexors and rotators, the glutes, and the front and back thigh muscles take over stabilization.

read more…show less

On top of that comes the core and back musculature (including the spinal erectors), which keeps the upper body upright where a backrest would otherwise provide support. These are exactly the muscle groups that, in the Hadza study mentioned on the homepage, were measured as noticeably more active in squatting and kneeling positions than in chair sitting.

Source: Raichlen et al., PNAS 2020 (primary source)

How quickly does the muscle get stronger?

From a training-physiology standpoint, strength gains can roughly be split into two phases: early improvements are mostly neural — better activation of the muscles involved, often noticeable within the first 2 to 4 weeks of regular practice.

read more…show less

Structural adaptations from actual muscle growth kick in later and usually only become measurable after 6 to 8 weeks of regular training. This holds for strength training in general and can reasonably be applied to the muscles worked by regular floor sitting — a hint that patience is part of the process.

Source: Moritani T, deVries HA (1979): „Neural factors versus hypertrophy in the time course of muscle strength gain." American Journal of Physical Medicine 58(3).

How long does it take to get used to new positions?

How quickly deeper positions like the squat or the kneeling seat start to feel comfortable varies a lot from person to person — with less mobile hips and ankles, it can take several weeks to months.

read more…show less

A gradual approach helps: short floor-sitting intervals within the PostureShift rotation that you build up over time, rather than sitting on the floor for long stretches from the start.

Practical note — not a study, our own assessment based on general training experience.

Sleeping too: a related idea

Floor sleeping is culturally common in the same regions as floor sitting — for example in Japan with the futon, or across large parts of Asia and Africa. The principle is similar: a firm, level surface instead of a tall, soft bed.

read more…show less

Sleep quality and the right mattress firmness are their own, highly individual topic, though. Some people prefer a firmer surface — but that doesn't add up to a blanket recommendation to sleep on the floor.

Source: Indojunkie

How common is floor sitting worldwide?

There's no reliable global percentage for "floor vs. chair use" — credible sources deliberately avoid such figures. What can be shown, though, is that in countries like India, Japan, and Korea, as well as large parts of Africa and Southeast Asia, floor sitting is simply part of everyday life.

read more…show less

Anthropologist Gordon W. Hewes documented more than 100 habitual sitting postures worldwide in his widely cited work — concluding that at least a quarter of humanity habitually rests in a deep squat.

Source: Rheumaliga Schweiz (citing Gordon W. Hewes)

Everyday movement instead of extra workouts

Among the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer community in Tanzania, movement built into daily life accounts for a large share of activity: roughly 135 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity per day, with no separate fitness routine at all.

read more…show less

This suggests that movement woven into everyday life can help make separate, compensatory exercise at least partly unnecessary — though it shouldn't be understood as a guarantee or a replacement for exercise, but rather as one building block among several.

Source: Spektrum der Wissenschaft, Raichlen et al., PNAS 2020 (primary source)

Why indigenous cultures never needed yoga or gymnastics

Neither yoga nor gymnastics originated as therapy for back pain: yoga began as a spiritual practice in India, while gymnastics in ancient Greece served military conditioning and aesthetics.

read more…show less

It wasn't until the 18th through 20th centuries that both were increasingly reinterpreted as a counterbalance to sedentary living. Cultures that never needed that reinterpretation simply had no use for a separate exercise program — because postural variety and movement were already woven into everyday life.

Editorial context — not a single source, our own historical summary.

The background

What we know about sitting, pain, and movement

"Sitting is the new smoking" has become a catchphrase — and there really is some research behind it. But a closer look at the key sources also shows: it's not about banning sitting altogether, but about how we sit and how often we change position.

"Sitting is the new smoking"

The phrase was coined by Mayo Clinic researcher Dr. James Levine. An important basis for it came from a study by the Pennington Biomedical Research Center using data from more than 17,000 Canadians: it found a link between long, uninterrupted daily sitting time and an increased mortality risk — regardless of how physically active people otherwise were in their leisure time. Regular breaks from sitting therefore appear to have an effect of their own, one that leisure-time exercise alone does not offset.

What the Hadza show us

A widely cited study by Raichlen, Pontzer, and colleagues (PNAS, 2020) examined the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer community in Tanzania, using movement sensors — and produced a surprising finding: the Hadza sit or rest for almost as long, in total, as people in industrialized nations. The difference lies not in the duration but in the posture. Resting positions such as deep squatting or kneeling keep the leg and core muscles noticeably more active than sitting in a chair does. The authors call this the "Inactivity Mismatch Hypothesis": our bodies are evolutionarily adapted to varyingly active resting postures — not to sitting motionless in one and the same position for hours on end.

Over 100 sitting positions worldwide

Cultural anthropology also shows that the chair is just one of many solutions. Anthropologist Gordon W. Hewes documented more than 100 distinct habitual sitting and resting postures from cultures around the globe in his widely cited study on the worldwide distribution of resting postures — from deep squatting among many African and Asian ethnic groups to distinct kneeling-sit forms such as among the Ainu in Japan or the sitting habits of North American peoples such as the Kaska in Canada. By comparison, the office chair as we know it is a young and relatively one-sided invention.

Some perspective: no need to fear the chair

Evolutionary biologist Daniel Lieberman (Harvard), who examines exactly this research in detail in his book "Exercised", also warns against overstating the case: sitting itself is a normal human behavior that has always been common, and it is not inherently harmful. It becomes a problem mainly when a single position is held for very long stretches without interruption.

Relax a little about sitting. Yes, it's good not to sit all day. It's good to move — but we shouldn't scare people about something as natural and normal as sitting. — paraphrased from Daniel Lieberman, "Exercised" (2021)

That balance is exactly the idea behind PostureShift: not prescribing one single "correct" posture, but regularly switching between differently active positions — at the pace and with the positions that fit your everyday life.

The solution

Regular change — in whatever form works for you

The core idea isn't one particular posture, but the timed switching between several. PostureShift gives you a freely extendable posture database and lets you build your own rotation from it — matched to your actual office setup.

Especially effective

Floor

Cross-legged sitting, kneeling ("seiza"), and other floor positions activate leg and core muscles the most — the posture that scores best in the research.

Chair

Ordinary sitting stays part of the rotation — what matters is that it isn't the only position for hours on end.

Bar stools & active seating

Saddle stools, wobble stools, and other active seating keep your core muscles engaged even while sitting.

Standing

At a standing desk or for a short walk in between — no standing desk required.

Floor sitting scores best in the research — that's why we highlight it. But not everyone can or wants to do that at the office: clothing, space constraints, colleagues, or the furniture situation often set limits. That's why PostureShift works just as well with a rotation made up solely of chair, bar stool, and standing — the switching itself is the decisive factor, not any one particular position.

Deep dive

Floor sitting in detail

Floor sitting scores especially well in the research. Here we go deeper: what sets it apart from other positions, how it fits into everyday life, and what the evidence across cultures suggests. Every claim links directly to its source.

What sets floor sitting apart from chairs, bar stools, and standing

The key difference isn't whether one position is "more active," but variety: on the floor you can shift between cross-legged sitting, kneeling, a deep squat, or outstretched legs without ever leaving your spot — a small shift in weight is enough.

read more…show less

With a chair, bar stool, or standing, on the other hand, a change of position almost always means switching furniture or the whole category. Historically, chair sitting also isn't a physically prescribed default: it tends to flatten the spine's natural curve and only became the cultural norm over time — not because it's anatomically necessary.

Source: Aktion Gesunder Rücken (AGR) e.V., Bewegte Schule: "Sitting on Chairs – The Origin Story of a Mass Phenomenon" (PDF)

Simple and inexpensive to adopt — even for kids

Floor sitting doesn't require any special furniture: a futon, a floor cushion (zafu), or a low table instead of standard dining height is enough. In countries like India, Japan, or Korea, sitting on the floor to eat is entirely normal.

read more…show less

Children naturally gravitate toward floor-level sitting postures on their own — a low table with cushions fits that natural preference better than a high chair with a footrest.

Source: Akutmag

Practical: barely any fall height

Floor furniture like cushions or a zafu has practically no fall height — an advantage over bar stools or tall chairs, especially in households with young children.

read more…show less

On smooth floors, a non-slip mat is worth using so cushions or seating surfaces don't slide out from under you.

Practical note — not a study, our own assessment.

Sleeping too: a related idea

Floor sleeping is culturally common in the same regions as floor sitting — for example in Japan with the futon, or across large parts of Asia and Africa. The principle is similar: a firm, level surface instead of a tall, soft bed.

read more…show less

Sleep quality and the right mattress firmness are their own, highly individual topic, though. Some people prefer a firmer surface — but that doesn't add up to a blanket recommendation to sleep on the floor.

Source: Indojunkie

How common is floor sitting worldwide?

There's no reliable global percentage for "floor vs. chair use" — credible sources deliberately avoid such figures. What can be shown, though, is that in countries like India, Japan, and Korea, as well as large parts of Africa and Southeast Asia, floor sitting is simply part of everyday life.

read more…show less

Anthropologist Gordon W. Hewes documented more than 100 habitual sitting postures worldwide in his widely cited work — concluding that at least a quarter of humanity habitually rests in a deep squat.

Source: Rheumaliga Schweiz (citing Gordon W. Hewes)

Everyday movement instead of extra workouts

Among the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer community in Tanzania, movement built into daily life accounts for a large share of activity: roughly 135 minutes of moderate to vigorous activity per day, with no separate fitness routine at all.

read more…show less

This suggests that movement woven into everyday life can help make separate, compensatory exercise at least partly unnecessary — though it shouldn't be understood as a guarantee or a replacement for exercise, but rather as one building block among several.

Source: Spektrum der Wissenschaft, Raichlen et al., PNAS 2020 (primary source)

Why indigenous cultures never needed yoga or gymnastics

Neither yoga nor gymnastics originated as therapy for back pain: yoga began as a spiritual practice in India, while gymnastics in ancient Greece served military conditioning and aesthetics.

read more…show less

It wasn't until the 18th through 20th centuries that both were increasingly reinterpreted as a counterbalance to sedentary living. Cultures that never needed that reinterpretation simply had no use for a separate exercise program — because postural variety and movement were already woven into everyday life.

Editorial context — not a single source, our own historical summary.

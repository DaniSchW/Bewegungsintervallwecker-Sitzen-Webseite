The background

What we know about sitting, pain, and movement

"Sitting is the new smoking" has become a catchphrase — and there really is some research behind it. But a closer look at the key sources also shows: it's not about banning sitting altogether, but about how we sit and how often we change position.

"Sitting is the new smoking"

The phrase was coined by Mayo Clinic researcher Dr. James Levine. An important basis for it came from a study by the Pennington Biomedical Research Center using data from more than 17,000 Canadians: it found a link between long, uninterrupted daily sitting time and an increased mortality risk.

This association held true regardless of how physically active people otherwise were in their leisure time. Regular breaks from sitting therefore appear to have an effect of their own, one that leisure-time exercise alone does not offset.

What the Hadza show us

A widely cited study by Raichlen, Pontzer, and colleagues (PNAS, 2020) examined the Hadza, a hunter-gatherer community in Tanzania, using movement sensors — and produced a surprising finding: the Hadza sit or rest for almost as long, in total, as people in industrialized nations.

read more…show less

The difference lies not in the duration but in the posture. Resting positions such as deep squatting or kneeling keep the leg and core muscles noticeably more active than sitting in a chair does. The authors call this the "Inactivity Mismatch Hypothesis": our bodies are evolutionarily adapted to varyingly active resting postures — not to sitting motionless in one and the same position for hours on end.

Over 100 sitting positions worldwide

Cultural anthropology also shows that the chair is just one of many solutions. Anthropologist Gordon W. Hewes documented more than 100 distinct habitual sitting and resting postures from cultures around the globe in his widely cited study on the worldwide distribution of resting postures.

read more…show less

From deep squatting among many African and Asian ethnic groups to distinct kneeling-sit forms such as among the Ainu in Japan or the sitting habits of North American peoples such as the Kaska in Canada. By comparison, the office chair as we know it is a young and relatively one-sided invention.

Some perspective: no need to fear the chair

Evolutionary biologist Daniel Lieberman (Harvard), who examines exactly this research in detail in his book "Exercised", also warns against overstating the case: sitting itself is a normal human behavior that has always been common, and it is not inherently harmful.

read more…show less

It becomes a problem mainly when a single position is held for very long stretches without interruption.

Relax a little about sitting. Yes, it's good not to sit all day. It's good to move — but we shouldn't scare people about something as natural and normal as sitting. — paraphrased from Daniel Lieberman, "Exercised" (2021)

That balance is exactly the idea behind PostureShift: not prescribing one single "correct" posture, but regularly switching between differently active positions — at the pace and with the positions that fit your everyday life.

The solution

Regular change — in whatever form works for you

The core idea isn't one particular posture, but the timed switching between several. PostureShift gives you a freely extendable posture database and lets you build your own rotation from it — matched to your actual office setup.

Especially effective

Floor

Cross-legged sitting, kneeling ("seiza"), and other floor positions activate leg and core muscles the most — the posture that scores best in the research.

Chair

Ordinary sitting stays part of the rotation — what matters is that it isn't the only position for hours on end.

Bar stools & active seating

Saddle stools, wobble stools, and other active seating keep your core muscles engaged even while sitting.

Standing

At a standing desk or for a short walk in between — no standing desk required.

Floor sitting scores best in the research — that's why we highlight it. But not everyone can or wants to do that at the office: clothing, space constraints, colleagues, or the furniture situation often set limits. That's why PostureShift works just as well with a rotation made up solely of chair, bar stool, and standing — the switching itself is the decisive factor, not any one particular position.
